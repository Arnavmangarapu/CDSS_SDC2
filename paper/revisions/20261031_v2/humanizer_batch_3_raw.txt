The provenance verifier accepted 26 out of 26 controls and rejected 132 out of 132 made-up changes. This checks if the source is real not whether the answers are clinically correct. Table 5 shows our tasks next to the ones in the base paper [20] for context. That study got 47 out of 50 correct on MedQA, 44 out of 50 on PubMedQA and 42 out of 50 on MedBullets. They used matching for MedQA and a free-text similarity score of 0.80 for the other two. Our ended diagnosis and medication-warning tasks used different questions, different methods and different scoring. So they don’t match up directly. We’re not saying our work is better or worse than theirs.

All the code, source files, fixed development steps and summary results are saved in a versioned research archive [16]. There’s no release, with this paper. We didn’t include raw exam text books, credentials, database backups or full model outputs. The experiments used public exam cases and fake cases written by developers. We didn’t collect any patient records. We also haven’t done clinical review or tested on held-out clinical data.
